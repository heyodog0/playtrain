## 1. What the 0.19M / 0.35M GPU-split numbers actually are

Section 4 said "0.19~M if the second GPU is left on inference instead of the
learner," which implies a two-GPU machine. The node has **four**. Measured, all
IMPALA-CNN, one node:

| learner GPUs | inference GPUs | workers | throughput | source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 3 (`cuda:1,2,3`) | 15 | **0.195M** | `outputs/suite_icnn_37707778.json` |
| 2 | 2 (`cuda:2,3`) | 12 | **0.351M** | `outputs/suite_icnn24_38338749.json`, `icnn_l2_37723136` |
| 3 | 1 (`cuda:3`) | 12 | **0.213M** | `outputs/icnn_l3_37723136.json` |

**Two-two is a genuine optimum, not a monotone trend.** Giving the learner a
third GPU drops throughput back to 0.21M, because inference becomes the
constraint. That is a stronger version of the "learner is the bottleneck"
sentence and is currently unused in the paper.

Caveat before quoting: the 1-learner row also uses 15 workers against the
others' 12, so it is not a clean single-variable swap.

Note `outputs/bench_train_suite_1gpu_36693963.json` is **Nature-CNN**, mean
0.137M. It is not the source of the 0.19M figure and should not be cited as it.

## 2. "Throughput barely varies by game" is true, with numbers

| encoder | n | mean | slowest | fastest | spread | CV |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| IMPALA-CNN | 24 | 0.351M | 0.341M `miner` | 0.360M `maze` | **1.06x** | **1.2%** |
| Nature-CNN | 8 | 1.090M | 0.564M `qbert` | 1.199M `seaquest` | 2.13x | 18.4% |

All 24 games fall within 6% of each other under IMPALA-CNN. The Nature-CNN
contrast is the evidence that this is a saturated learner rather than a property
of the trainer, so the encoder must stay named in that sentence. `miner` being
the slowest here also lines up with it being the one ProcGen replica we step
slower than the original.

Source: `outputs/suite_icnn24_38338749.json`, `outputs/suite_nat_ddp2_37717892.json`.

## 4. Paper notes from this session

- Gymnasium citation added at the validation-script sentence (`a54d8d1`). The bib
  already had `towers2024gymnasium`; no new entry was needed.
- **Terminology is inconsistent for one concept**: `draw call` x6,
  `drawing command` x5, `draw command` x2, `drawing call` x2. `draw call` is the
  one used in the schematic caption and in the rasterizer sentence, so it is the
  natural target for a unifying pass.
- The three-step draw pipeline sentence maps onto the three components (QuickJS,
  the C++ p5 layer, the Rust rasterizer), so all three steps are load-bearing.
  Its framing clause is off: it promises to describe turning *draw commands* into
  pixels, but step 1 is the code running, which is what produces them.
- The claim "LLMs are very good at editing and correcting JS code" has no
  supporting citation and the bib contains **no** code-generation or code-repair
  entries at all. The better support is first-party and already in the paper:
  26 model calls, 34.4 minutes, $0.95 across six artifacts (`tab:llm-cost`).
  Standard code benchmarks are Python-centric and a poor fit for a JS claim.
